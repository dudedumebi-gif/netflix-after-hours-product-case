# Phase 1 Research Charter

## 1. Purpose

Phase 1 will determine whether late-night streaming creates a sufficiently frequent and meaningful customer problem, whether the proposed experience addresses it, and whether the resulting behaviour could support a credible economic case.

The research will validate the problem before validating the full solution. It will not treat existing portfolio projections as customer evidence.

## 2. Decisions this research must support

1. Should Netflix After Hours proceed to a focused pilot?
2. Which customer segment experiences the strongest problem?
3. Which smallest feature combination produces a meaningful customer benefit?
4. Which features belong in the pilot, later releases, or the parking lot?
5. Which behavioural outcomes can serve as economic proxies?
6. What privacy, accessibility, household, or technical risks could prevent adoption?

## 3. Primary research questions

### Problem and behaviour

- How often do viewers fall asleep while streaming?
- Which devices, content types, times, and household settings are involved?
- How much unintended playback occurs after the viewer falls asleep?
- How often do viewers lose their true stopping point, encounter spoilers, or manually search for the correct position?
- What workarounds do viewers use today?

### Customer value

- Which problem matters most: viewing continuity, screen light, disruptive audio, battery use, household disturbance, or wasted playback?
- Does the proposed timer and recovery journey feel useful enough to adopt repeatedly?
- Do viewers prefer manual control, schedule-based automation, inactivity detection, or wearable detection?
- What makes the experience feel helpful rather than intrusive?

### Usability and trust

- Can viewers discover and configure the timer without assistance?
- Do viewers understand when dimming begins, when playback stops, and how to cancel?
- Does the recovery card clearly explain the saved point?
- How should the product behave when another household member is still watching?
- What health-data permissions would viewers accept, and under what conditions?

### Economic value

- How many unattended playback minutes could the pilot prevent?
- Could successful recovery and fewer spoilers improve satisfaction or retention intent?
- Which advanced capability has credible Premium upgrade potential?
- Which assumptions drive the economic model most strongly?

## 4. Research population

### Core inclusion criteria

Participants must:

- Be 18 years or older
- Use a paid or household streaming service at least weekly
- Watch video after 9:00 PM at least twice per week
- Have fallen asleep, become drowsy, or intentionally used streaming to wind down within the past three months

### Segments

1. Habitual bedtime viewers using TV or mobile
2. Late-night series viewers concerned about continuity or spoilers
3. Audio-first or wearable-owning viewers interested in automation
4. Shared-screen household viewers who may remain awake when another viewer sleeps
5. Shift workers or travellers whose bedtime occurs outside conventional night hours

## 5. Method plan

### Secondary research

Review current evidence on late-night streaming behaviour, sleep-timer adoption, streaming continuity, wearable permissions, household viewing, and relevant competitor capabilities. Public evidence must be captured in a claim-and-source register with publication date, population, method, limitations, and relevance.

### Quantitative survey

- **Target:** 300 qualified respondents
- **Acceptable range:** 200-400
- **Purpose:** Estimate problem frequency, segment differences, current workarounds, feature demand, privacy acceptance, and willingness to consider Premium automation
- **Key cuts:** Device, household type, viewing frequency, content type, plan type, wearable ownership, bedtime pattern, and country or region

### Qualitative interviews

- **Target:** 12-15 participants
- **Allocation:** At least four participants from each primary persona group, with shared-screen and shift-worker behaviours represented across the sample
- **Purpose:** Reconstruct the actual bedtime journey, reveal workarounds and emotional consequences, understand language, and identify trust or household risks

### Prototype usability testing

- **Round 1:** 5-8 participants
- **Round 2:** 5-8 participants after iteration
- **Critical tasks:** Find the timer, set an end condition, understand dimming, cancel an unwanted transition, resume from the saved point, and interpret wearable permissions

## 6. Pre-committed Gate 2 decision thresholds

These thresholds are proposed for Gate 1 approval and may be revised before data collection. They must not be changed after results are visible without documenting the reason.

| Dimension | Proposed threshold |
| --- | --- |
| Problem frequency | At least 30% of qualified respondents report falling asleep while streaming at least twice per month |
| Continuity impact | At least 50% of affected respondents report unintended episode progression, lost position, or manual searching |
| Problem severity | At least 40% of affected respondents rate the experience as moderately or highly frustrating |
| Core desirability | At least 60% of affected respondents say they would probably or definitely use a manual timer plus recovery experience |
| Pilot task success | At least 80% complete each critical task without moderator intervention by usability Round 2 |
| Intrusion guardrail | No more than 10% of usability participants misunderstand how to cancel or believe playback could stop against their intent |
| Economic signal | Conservative scenario shows a plausible positive contribution through retention, Premium conversion, or avoided unattended playback |

These thresholds guide a portfolio case decision. They do not represent Netflix internal standards.

## 7. Evidence standards

Each material statement will be labelled as one of the following:

- **Assumption:** A condition accepted temporarily to enable analysis
- **Hypothesis:** A falsifiable prediction to be tested
- **Target:** A desired future performance level
- **Benchmark:** A comparison derived from an identified external source
- **Finding:** An observed research result with method and sample disclosed
- **Validated finding:** A finding that passes the defined evidence and quality criteria

No simulated participant response may be reported as customer evidence. AI may assist with instrument design, coding, synthesis, and quality checks, but human participant data must remain identifiable as actual collected data.

## 8. Research ethics and privacy

- Obtain informed consent before interviews or usability sessions.
- Collect only information required for the research questions.
- Avoid collecting health records or raw wearable data.
- Separate participant identifiers from response data.
- Report aggregated results and redact identifying details.
- Make clear that the project is independent and not affiliated with Netflix.
- Avoid medical claims about sleep improvement or binaural audio effectiveness.

## 9. Key limitations

- Participants will represent general streaming users rather than verified Netflix internal cohorts.
- Netflix-specific infrastructure costs and subscriber economics are unavailable.
- Stated willingness to pay does not equal observed conversion.
- Prototype usability does not establish production feasibility.
- Survey estimates depend on recruitment quality and sample composition.

## 10. Gate 1 approval criteria

Gate 1 is complete when the product owner approves:

1. Research questions and participant criteria
2. Proposed decision thresholds
3. Evidence classifications and claim-handling rules
4. Research methods and sample ranges
5. Economic value pathways
6. Ethical and portfolio disclaimers
